Lesson 001: Why AI Lyrics Fall Flat

Lesson 1 of the series Estimated time: 15 minutes
Mission connection: You're making music with Suno but the AI-written lyrics feel generic. Before you can write better lyrics, you need to understand what specifically makes lyrics work or fail. This lesson gives you that diagnostic lens.

The Problem You Already Feel

You've pasted a prompt into Suno or ChatGPT and gotten lyrics back. They rhyme. They have verses and a chorus. They're grammatically correct. And yet they feel... flat. Interchangeable. Like they could belong to any song about any topic.

You're not imagining it. There's a specific, diagnosable reason AI lyrics underwhelm. It's the same reason most human beginner lyrics underwhelm. And once you can name it, you can fix it — in your own writing or in AI drafts you're editing.

The concept is called prosody.

What Is Prosody?

Prosody is the single most important concept in lyric writing. It means: every element of your song should support the same emotion.

"Prosody is the appropriate match of all the elements of a song — lyrics, melody, harmony, rhythm, and structure — to support and enhance the central idea or emotion."
— Pat Pattison, Songwriting: Writing the Lyrics (Berklee / Coursera)

When a sad lyric sits on a bouncy melody, something feels wrong. When a triumphant chorus uses weak, uncertain words, it deflates. When a line about freedom is trapped in a tight, short rhythmic box, the listener's gut says "this doesn't work" — even if they can't articulate why.

That mismatch is broken prosody. And it's the #1 disease of AI-generated lyrics.

Why AI Breaks Prosody

AI language models generate lyrics by predicting the most statistically likely next word. They're very good at surface patterns: rhyme, syllable count, song structure. But they have no concept of what the song feels like when sung. They can't hear the music. They don't know whether the melody rises or falls, whether the rhythm is driving or floating.

This means AI lyrics tend to:

  • Use generic emotional language — words like "heart," "fire," "soul," "night" that fit anywhere and say nothing specific
  • Ignore rhythmic feel — syllable stress patterns that fight the beat instead of riding it
  • Rhyme for rhyme's sake — choosing words because they rhyme, not because they advance the meaning
  • Stay at the same emotional altitude — no tension, no release, no journey from verse to chorus

Let's see this in action.

Diagnosis: A Real Example

Here's a typical AI-generated verse and chorus for an indie-pop song about missing someone:

AI-Generated (Weak Prosody)

Verse I think about you every single night The memories we shared were shining bright My heart is heavy and I can't let go I wonder if you feel it too, you know Chorus I'm falling, falling into the dark You left a wound upon my heart But I'll keep holding on to hope I know that somehow I'll learn to cope

On the surface, this looks like a song. It rhymes (AABB). It has a verse and chorus. It's "about" missing someone. But look closer:

  • Every line is the same length. There's no rhythmic variety. No line feels urgent; no line stretches out with longing. The emotional temperature is flat.
  • The rhymes are predictable. Night/bright, dark/heart, hope/cope — these are the first rhymes anyone would think of. They carry no surprise, no discovery.
  • It tells instead of showing. "My heart is heavy" is a label for an emotion, not an experience of it. What does heaviness actually feel like to this person?
  • The chorus doesn't lift. A chorus should feel like arrival — higher energy, a core truth crystallized. This chorus just... continues the same mood at the same altitude.
  • "You know" is padding. It exists only to fill the meter. The listener can feel that.

Now compare:

Human-Written (Strong Prosody)

Verse Your coffee cup's still on the shelf ← concrete image I keep on talking to myself ← specific behavior, not a label The kitchen radio plays our song And I don't turn it off Chorus So come home ← short, punchy — prosodic match to urgency Come home The light I left on's burning low ← image does double duty: literal + metaphor Come home

Notice the differences:

  • Concrete images. Coffee cup, kitchen radio, a light left on. You can see this scene. The emotion isn't stated — it's shown through objects and actions.
  • Line lengths vary. The short "And I don't turn it off" breaks the pattern and creates a catch in the throat. The chorus's "Come home" is two syllables — it punches.
  • The chorus is a different altitude. The verse is quiet observation. The chorus is raw plea. That contrast is prosody in action.
  • Rhyme is sparse and purposeful. Not every line rhymes — so when "low" arrives, it lands with weight.

The Four Prosody Levers

Pat Pattison's framework (from the Coursera course and his book Writing Better Lyrics) identifies four main tools you can use to create or break prosody in lyrics. We'll explore each in future lessons, but here's the map:

Lever What it controls Example
Number of lines Stability vs. instability of a section Even number of lines (2, 4) feels resolved; odd (3, 5) feels unfinished — pushing forward
Line length Pace, urgency, breathing room Short lines = tension, urgency. Long lines = expansion, storytelling
Rhyme scheme How "found" or "lost" the listener feels AABB = stable, resolved. ABAB = moderate motion. XAXA = open, searching
Rhythm (stress pattern) How naturally the words sit in the music Stressed syllables should land on strong beats. "forGET" on a downbeat works; "FORget" doesn't

These four levers are your diagnostic toolkit. When lyrics feel wrong, one or more of these is mismatched with the song's emotional intent.

Your Suno workflow

When you generate lyrics with AI for Suno, run them through the four levers before pasting them in. Ask: Are the line lengths varied? Are the rhymes predictable? Does the chorus feel different from the verse? Is it showing or telling? Even fixing one of these will improve the result.

Your First Exercise

Here's what to do before our next lesson:

  1. Generate a set of lyrics with AI (ChatGPT, Claude, or Suno's built-in generator). Any topic, any genre you like.
  2. Diagnose them using the four prosody levers. For each lever, write one sentence about what the AI did well or poorly.
  3. Rewrite the chorus. Just the chorus. Try to: use at least one concrete image, vary the line lengths, and make the chorus feel different from the verse.

Don't worry about it being good. The goal is to start seeing lyrics as a set of craft decisions, not just words.

Key takeaway: Lyrics aren't just words that rhyme — they're a set of structural and sensory choices that either support or undercut the song's emotion. AI misses this because it optimizes for surface patterns. You can do better by learning to hear what's underneath.
