Lesson 005: Writing Your First Song

Lesson 5 of the series Estimated time: 30 minutes (includes writing)
Mission connection: This is it. You've learned to diagnose lyrics (Lessons 1–4). Now you write. By the end of this lesson, you'll have a complete set of lyrics — verse, chorus, and bridge — ready to paste into Suno. No AI drafts. Just you and the four levers.

The Process

There is no single "right" way to write a song. But there is a process that prevents blank-page paralysis and keeps you from writing in circles. Here's the one we'll use, adapted from Pat Pattison's approach in the Coursera course (Module 1: "Boxes — Your Development Engine"):

  1. Find your seed — a title, a phrase, or an image
  2. Mine it — sense-mining to generate raw material
  3. Map the journey — decide where the song starts and where it ends up
  4. Choose your structure — how many sections, what shape
  5. Write the chorus first — the destination, not the departure
  6. Write the verse — the path to the chorus
  7. Write the bridge — the turn
  8. Audit with the four levers — diagnose and fix

We'll walk through each step. Do each step as you read it — don't just read ahead. The lesson is designed as a guided writing session. Grab something to write with.

Step 1: Find Your Seed

A seed is the starting point — a phrase, image, or title that contains emotional potential. It doesn't need to be the final title. It just needs to make you feel something.

Some ways to find seeds:

  • A phrase you overheard — something someone said that stuck with you
  • A concrete image — (Lesson 2) something you saw that carried emotion: an empty parking lot at night, a text message left on read, shoes by the door
  • A contradiction — two feelings that don't belong together: "happy to be sad," "comfortable alone," "the freedom of giving up"
  • A question — "What if I never called back?" "Was it worth it?" "Do you think about me?"
Do it now

Write down 3–5 seeds. Don't overthink. Spend 2 minutes. Pick the one that makes you feel the most.

Step 2: Mine It

Take your seed and do 5 minutes of sense-mining (Lesson 2). Write down everything the seed connects to through your five senses:

  • See: What does this look like? What's the scene? Time of day? What objects are in the frame?
  • Hear: What sounds are present? Silence? Traffic? A song on the radio?
  • Touch: What's the physical sensation? Cold hands? A phone screen? Bedsheets?
  • Smell/Taste: Coffee? Rain? Smoke? Something cooking?
  • Action: What is someone doing? Driving? Scrolling? Staring at a ceiling?

Write continuously. Don't edit. Don't judge. You'll get 20 fragments and maybe 5 good ones. That's perfect.

Do it now

Set a timer for 5 minutes. Mine your seed. Write everything that comes, even if it's messy.

Step 3: Map the Journey

A song is a journey — it starts in one emotional place and ends in another. The listener should feel moved, literally: transported from point A to point B.

Pattison calls this the "development engine" — a simple box diagram:

┌─────────────────┐ ┌─────────────────┐ ┌─────────────────┐ │ VERSE 1 │ → │ CHORUS │ │ BRIDGE │ │ Where it │ │ The core │ │ The turn │ │ starts │ │ truth │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ Situation. │ │ What it │ │ New angle. │ │ Setting. │ │ means. │ │ Shift. │ │ Who/where/when │ │ How it feels. │ │ Surprise. │ └─────────────────┘ └─────────────────┘ └─────────────────┘ ↑ │ ┌─────────────────┐ │ │ │ VERSE 2 │ ────────┘ │ │ Development. │ │ │ Deeper. │ ┌─────────────────┐ │ │ Complication. │ │ FINAL CHORUS │ ←───┘ └─────────────────┘ │ Hits different │ │ now. │ └─────────────────┘

Fill in your boxes with one sentence each:

  • Verse 1: Where does the song start? What's the situation? (Example: "I'm driving past your house at night, alone for the first time.")
  • Chorus: What's the core emotional truth? The thing you need to say? (Example: "I still want you but I know I shouldn't come back.")
  • Verse 2: How does the situation deepen or complicate? (Example: "I see your light on and imagine you with someone else.")
  • Bridge: What's the turn — a new angle, a realization, a shift? (Example: "Maybe the problem was never you. Maybe I just can't be alone.")
Do it now

Write your four boxes. One sentence each. This is your map — don't start writing lyrics until you have it.

Step 4: Choose Your Structure

For your first song, use this standard structure. It works for pop, indie, and R&B:

Section Lines Stability Rhyme approach
Verse 1 5–6 lines Unstable (odd preferred, or varied lengths) Loose — assonance, ABCB or XAXA
Chorus 4 lines Stable (even count, matched lengths) Tighter — perfect or family rhyme, AABB or ABAB
Verse 2 5–6 lines Unstable (same shape as Verse 1) Same scheme as Verse 1
Chorus (repeat)
Bridge 3–4 lines Different from both verse and chorus Break the pattern — new scheme or rhyme type
Final Chorus (repeat, maybe with a small variation)

This is a starting point, not a cage. Feel free to add a pre-chorus, change the line counts, or drop the bridge if it doesn't serve the song. But having a structure before you write prevents the most common beginner mistake: writing a formless blob of lines with no sections.

Step 5: Write the Chorus First

This feels counterintuitive — shouldn't you start at the beginning? No. The chorus is your destination. If you don't know where you're going, the verses will wander.

Your chorus should:

  • State the core truth from your box diagram — but in concrete, specific language (not telling)
  • Be 4 lines (stable — Lesson 3)
  • Use tighter rhyme than the verse (Lesson 4)
  • Contain your title (or hook phrase) — usually the first or last line
  • Feel like an arrival — higher energy, more direct, than the verse

Pull from your sense-mining material. Find the strongest image or phrase and build the chorus around it.

Do it now

Write your chorus. 4 lines. Include your title/hook. Use at least one concrete image. Don't try to be perfect — get something down and we'll refine.

Step 6: Write the Verse

Now work backwards: what scene, situation, or moment leads to the chorus? The verse is the setup. It earns the chorus.

Your verse should:

  • Set the scene — who, where, when (concrete images)
  • Be 5–6 lines (odd preferred — unstable, pushing forward)
  • Use looser rhyme than the chorus — assonance, ABCB, or scattered
  • Vary line lengths — at least one short line among longer ones
  • End on a line that leans into the chorus — the last verse line should feel incomplete, making the chorus's arrival feel necessary
Do it now

Write Verse 1. 5–6 lines. Set the scene. End on a line that makes the chorus feel like it has to come next. Then write Verse 2 — same structure, different content (deeper, more complicated).

Step 7: Write the Bridge

The bridge is the turn — a new perspective that recontextualizes the chorus when it returns for the final time. It should feel structurally different from both verse and chorus.

Common bridge moves:

  • Change the "I" — shift from talking about them to talking about yourself (or vice versa)
  • Zoom out — from the specific moment to the big picture
  • Admit something — the thing you've been avoiding saying
  • Contradict the chorus — say the opposite, then let the final chorus answer it

Keep it short: 3–4 lines. Use a different rhyme scheme or type than the verse and chorus. If the verse uses ABCB and the chorus uses AABB, try the bridge with no rhyme, or a single couplet.

Do it now

Write your bridge. 3–4 lines. Make it different. Then read the whole song aloud: Verse 1 → Chorus → Verse 2 → Chorus → Bridge → Chorus.

Step 8: Audit with the Four Levers

You have a draft. Now put on your diagnostic hat and run it through the four levers from Lesson 1:

Lever Question to ask Fix if needed
Content Are there any "telling" lines? Any emotion-labels without a concrete image? Replace with a specific image using sense-mining (Lesson 2)
Line count Is the verse unstable (odd)? Is the chorus stable (even)? Is there contrast? Add or cut a line to change the count (Lesson 3)
Line length Are all lines the same length? Is there at least one surprise — a short punch or a long sprawl? Shorten or extend one line for contrast (Lesson 3)
Rhyme Is every rhyme perfect? Is the scheme AABB throughout? Does the chorus rhyme more tightly than the verse? Loosen verse rhymes to assonance. Change scheme. (Lesson 4)
Read it aloud

Always read your lyrics out loud — or better, sing them, even badly. Lyrics are meant to be heard, not read. If a line trips your tongue, the rhythm is off. If you run out of breath, the line is too long. If a word feels forced, it probably is. Your mouth knows before your brain does.

Step 9: Format for Suno

Suno reads section labels in square brackets. Format your finished lyrics like this:

[Verse 1] Your verse 1 lines here ... [Chorus] Your chorus lines here ... [Verse 2] Your verse 2 lines here ... [Chorus] Your chorus lines here ... [Bridge] Your bridge lines here ... [Chorus] Your chorus lines here (optional: small variation on the final chorus)

Paste this into Suno, pick a genre tag that matches your intent (indie pop, alternative rock, R&B, etc.), and generate. Listen to the result. Then come back and adjust — you'll hear things in the music that you couldn't hear on paper.

The iteration loop

Writing lyrics is not a one-pass process. Professional songwriters rewrite constantly. Your first draft is raw material. Generate it in Suno, listen, note what feels wrong, come back to the lyrics and apply the four levers again. Each pass gets closer.

Example: A Complete Walkthrough

Here's what the process might look like in practice, from seed to finished lyrics:

Seed

"Left the porch light on" — an image of waiting for someone who isn't coming back.

Sense-mining (2 minutes, unedited)

Yellow glow through curtains. Moths bumping the bulb. Electric bill climbing. Neighbors can see I'm still up. Front door unlocked. Cold mug on the step. Dew on the railing. That click when the bulb finally dies. Shadow of the mailbox. Driveway empty. Porch swing creaking with nobody in it. The sound of nothing coming up the walk.

Box diagram

V1: I still leave the porch light on every night, even though you're gone.
Chorus: The light's still on but nobody's coming home.
V2: I notice small signs I'm holding on — your mug, the unlocked door.
Bridge: Maybe I'm not leaving it on for you. Maybe I'm afraid of the dark.

Finished lyrics

Verse 1 Moths are tapping on the glass again ← concrete image, sets the scene I count them like the nights Since you took your jacket off the hook ← showing, not "since you left" Porch swing's rocking on its own ← short line, image does double duty And I still leave the light on Chorus But nobody's driving up the road Nobody's coming home ← assonance (road/home — /oʊ/ vowel) I'm just a porch light burning With nobody left to hold ← assonance chain continues Verse 2 Your coffee mug's still on the step I rinse it every morning out of habit The front door hasn't locked in weeks ← "habit / weeks" — no rhyme, conversational Like something in me thinks You'll walk back through Bridge Maybe it was never for you ← the turn: recontextualizes everything Maybe I just can't stand the dark Maybe I leave it on for me ← 3 lines, odd = unstable, unresolved Final Chorus But nobody's driving up the road Nobody's coming home I'm just a porch light burning With nobody left to hold

Let's audit this with the four levers:

  • Content: All showing — moths, jacket, porch swing, coffee mug, unlocked door. No emotion-labels.
  • Line count: Verses are 5 lines (odd = unstable). Chorus is 4 (even = stable). Bridge is 3 (odd = unstable, leaning into final chorus). Contrast between sections.
  • Line length: Varied. "Porch swing's rocking on its own" is shorter than the lines around it. "You'll walk back through" is very short — a gut punch after the longer lines above.
  • Rhyme: Verse uses scattered assonance. Chorus uses assonance (road/home/hold). Bridge has no rhyme. The verse is looser than the chorus. Different scheme for the bridge.
Key takeaway: Writing lyrics is a process, not a flash of inspiration. Seed → mine → map → structure → chorus first → verse → bridge → audit. Each step is small and manageable. The four levers from Lessons 1–4 are your quality control. And the first draft is never the final one — iterate, listen in Suno, and revise.

Your Assignment

Write a complete song using the 8-step process above. Here are your requirements:

  1. At least 2 verses (5–6 lines each), 1 chorus (4 lines), and 1 bridge (3–4 lines)
  2. At least 3 concrete images across the song (no telling without showing)
  3. Structural contrast: verse and chorus should have different line counts
  4. At least one line-length surprise (a short line among longer ones)
  5. The chorus should rhyme more tightly than the verses

When you're done, format it with [Verse 1], [Chorus], etc., paste it into Suno, and generate a track. Listen. Then bring the lyrics and your reactions to our next session — I'll help you refine.
