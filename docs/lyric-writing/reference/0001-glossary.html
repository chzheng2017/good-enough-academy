Lyric Writing Glossary

Core terms used across all lessons. Updated as new concepts are introduced.

Term Definition Introduced
Prosody The match between all elements of a song (lyrics, melody, harmony, rhythm, structure) and the central emotion or idea. When everything supports the same feeling, prosody is strong. When elements fight each other, prosody is broken. Lesson 001
Showing vs. Telling Telling labels an emotion directly ("I'm sad"). Showing evokes it through concrete images, actions, or sensory details ("Your coffee cup's still on the shelf"). Showing is almost always more powerful. Lesson 001
Verse A section of a song that typically advances the story or develops an idea. Verses usually have different lyrics each time they appear but share the same melody and structure. Lesson 001
Chorus The repeated, central section of a song — its emotional peak and main message. Usually the same (or nearly the same) lyrics and melody each time. Should feel like an "arrival" — a different altitude from the verse. Lesson 001
Bridge A contrasting section that appears once, usually after the second chorus. Provides a new perspective, key change, or emotional turn before the final chorus. Lesson 001
Rhyme scheme The pattern of rhyming line endings in a section, notated with letters. AABB = consecutive rhymes (couplets). ABAB = alternating rhymes. XAXA = only even lines rhyme. Different schemes create different feelings of resolution. Lesson 001
Stability / Instability A section feels stable (resolved, settled) or unstable (unfinished, pushing forward) based on structural choices: number of lines, line lengths, rhyme scheme, rhythm. Verses are typically unstable (pushing toward the chorus); choruses are typically stable (arriving). Lesson 001
Line length How many syllables or beats a lyric line contains. Varying line lengths creates rhythmic interest and emotional contrast. Uniform line lengths feel flat. Lesson 001
Stressed / Unstressed syllable In English, every multi-syllable word has syllables that are naturally emphasized (stressed) or soft (unstressed). "forGET" — "for" is unstressed, "GET" is stressed. In lyrics, stressed syllables should align with strong beats in the music. Lesson 001
Concrete image A specific, sensory detail the listener can see, hear, touch, taste, or smell. "Coffee cup on the shelf" is concrete. "Memories we shared" is abstract. Concrete images make lyrics vivid and emotionally resonant. Lesson 001
Object writing / Sense-mining A brainstorming technique: pick an object or emotion, set a timer, and write everything it connects to through the five senses (sight, sound, touch, smell, taste) plus physical movement. Generates the raw material of concrete images. From Pat Pattison's Writing Better Lyrics. Lesson 002
Enjambment Breaking a phrase across two lines so the meaning carries over the line break. Creates suspense or emphasis. Example: "she's touching his— / Chest now" in Mr. Brightside. Lesson 002
The filmmaker test A quick check for concreteness: could a filmmaker shoot this line? If a camera can capture it, it's concrete (showing). If not, it's abstract (telling). Lesson 002
Structural stability A section feels stable (resolved, at rest) when it has an even number of lines and matched line lengths. It feels unstable (unfinished, pushing forward) with an odd number of lines or mismatched line lengths. Verses are typically unstable; choruses typically stable. Lesson 003
Pre-chorus A short transitional section between verse and chorus that builds tension. Often just 1–2 lines. Its instability creates forward momentum into the chorus landing. Example: the single-line pre-chorus in Billie Eilish's "when the party's over." Lesson 003
Structural contrast The difference in shape (line count, line lengths) between sections. Without structural contrast, the song feels flat — the chorus doesn't feel like an arrival because it has the same shape as the verse. AI lyrics almost always lack this. Lesson 003
Perfect rhyme Words where the vowel sound and all consonants after it match exactly: night/bright, heart/apart. Creates maximum sonic resolution (stability). The default for AI lyrics — and the reason they sound predictable. Lesson 004
Family rhyme Words where the vowel matches and the final consonants are from the same phonetic family (voiced/unvoiced pairs like d/t, b/p, g/k): bread/breath. Almost as resolved as perfect rhyme but fresher. Lesson 004
Assonance rhyme Words where the vowel sounds match but the consonants don't: time/alive/ride (all share the /aɪ/ vowel). Feels open, fluid, searching — the ear hears a connection but isn't locked in. Common in indie, pop, and R&B. Lesson 004
Consonance rhyme Words where the consonants match but the vowels don't: bread/brood, back/book. Feels dark, tense — the sounds echo but don't fully resolve. Lesson 004
Internal rhyme Rhyme that occurs within a line rather than at the end: "He left no time to regret / Kept his dick wet." Adds rhythmic density and drive without being as obvious as end rhyme. Lesson 004
Seed The starting point for a song — a phrase, image, question, or contradiction that contains emotional potential. Not necessarily the final title. Examples: "left the porch light on," "shoes by the door," "happy to be sad." Lesson 005
Development engine (Boxes) Pat Pattison's technique for mapping a song's emotional journey before writing. Each section (verse 1, chorus, verse 2, bridge) gets a one-sentence summary of its role. Prevents aimless writing and ensures the song goes somewhere. Lesson 005
Hook The most memorable phrase in the song — usually the title, usually in the chorus (first or last line). The line the listener walks away humming. The anchor of the entire song. Lesson 005
