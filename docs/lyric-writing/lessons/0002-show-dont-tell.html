Lesson 002: Show, Don't Tell

Lesson 2 of the series Estimated time: 15 minutes
Mission connection: The #1 way AI lyrics fall flat is by telling you how to feel instead of showing you. This lesson teaches you the single most powerful rewriting technique: replacing abstract emotion-labels with concrete, sensory details. After this, you'll be able to take any AI draft and make it land.

The Disease

Read these lines out loud:

Telling (Abstract)

I'm so sad without you here My heart is broken and I shed a tear I feel so lonely every night I miss the love that felt so right

These lines label emotions: sad, broken, lonely, miss. They're a report about feelings, not an experience of them. The listener's brain processes them as information — "this person is sad" — and moves on. There's nothing to feel.

This is what AI does almost every time. It's been trained on millions of lyrics, so it knows the vocabulary of emotion. But vocabulary isn't experience.

The Cure: Concrete Images

A concrete image is something you can perceive with your senses — something you can see, hear, touch, smell, or taste. When you replace an emotion-label with a concrete image, you force the listener to reconstruct the feeling themselves. That's what makes a lyric hit.

The job of the lyricist is not to say how you feel, but to make the listener feel it.

Let's see this in real songs you know.

Case Study 1: The Killers — "Mr. Brightside"

Brandon Flowers wrote this about discovering his girlfriend was cheating on him. He could have written "I'm jealous and it's tearing me apart." Instead:

The Killers — showing jealousy through action

Verse Now I'm falling asleep and she's calling a cab ← two simultaneous actions, no emotion-label While he's having a smoke and she's taking a drag ← shared cigarette = intimacy, shown not stated Now they're goin' to bed and my stomach is sick ← physical sensation, not "I feel jealous" And it's all in my head, but she's touching his— Pre-Chorus Chest now ← the line break is the anguish — he can't even finish the sentence He takes off her dress now Let me go

(Full lyrics on Genius)

Notice what Flowers does:

  • Actions, not adjectives. "Calling a cab," "having a smoke," "taking a drag," "touching his chest," "takes off her dress." Every line is a verb. The listener watches the scene unfold like a movie.
  • One physical sensation instead of an emotion-label. "My stomach is sick" — not "I feel jealous" or "I'm heartbroken." You've felt a sick stomach. Your body remembers. That's the bridge between the song and the listener's own experience.
  • The enjambment. "She's touching his— / Chest now" — the line break forces a pause right where the pain is. The word you expected doesn't arrive on time. That's prosody: structure matching emotion.
The filmmaker test

Read each line and ask: could a filmmaker shoot this? If a camera can capture it, it's concrete. "She's calling a cab" — yes, a camera can show that. "My heart is broken" — no. A camera can't film a metaphorical broken heart. That's the line between showing and telling.

Case Study 2: Adele — "Someone Like You"

This song is mostly telling — and it works because Adele's voice carries so much raw emotion that it transcends the lyrics. But look at where the lyrics are strongest:

Verse 1 — mostly telling I heard that you're settled down ← reporting information That you found a girl and you're married now ← reporting information I heard that your dreams came true ← abstract Guess she gave you things I didn't give to you ← abstract, but personal — "I" vs. "she"
Verse 2 — the strongest moment You know how the time flies ← cliché, but sets up... Only yesterday was the time of our lives ← "only yesterday" — time compression creates ache We were born and raised in a summer haze ← CONCRETE. You can feel summer haze. Bound by the surprise of our glory days ← less concrete, but "bound" is physical

(Full lyrics on Genius)

"Summer haze" is the strongest image in the entire song. You can feel the heat, the hazy light, the lazy afternoons. It does more emotional work than any of the explicit statements like "I wish nothing but the best for you."

Lesson

Even one strong concrete image in an otherwise abstract song can anchor the whole thing. You don't need every line to be a sensory detail — but you need enough to give the listener something to hold onto. Aim for at least one per section (verse, chorus, bridge).

The Technique: Sense-Mining

Here's a practical method for generating concrete images. Pat Pattison calls a version of this "object writing" (from Writing Better Lyrics, Chapter 1). The simplified version:

  1. Start with the emotion. What's the song about? Loneliness? Euphoria? Regret?
  2. Ask: what does this emotion look like? Not metaphorically — literally. When you last felt this way, what were you looking at? An empty chair? Rain on a window? A phone screen with no notifications?
  3. Ask: what does it sound like? Silence in a room that used to have music? The hum of a refrigerator? Traffic at 3 AM?
  4. Ask: what does it feel physically? Tight chest? Cold hands? A weight behind the eyes?
  5. Ask: what does it smell or taste like? Cold coffee? Cigarette smoke on a jacket? Rain on asphalt?

Write down everything, without filtering. You'll get 20 bad ideas and 3 great ones. The 3 great ones are your lyrics.

Practice: Rewrite These AI Lines

Here are five AI-generated "telling" lines. For each one, write a "showing" replacement using a concrete image. There's no single right answer — the goal is to practice the swap.

# AI Line (Telling) Your Rewrite (Showing)
1 "I'm so lonely without you here" Write yours — what does lonely look like in a specific room?

Key takeaway: Don't tell the listener what to feel — give them something to see, hear, or touch, and let the feeling arise on its own. One concrete image does more emotional work than ten abstract statements. This is the single fastest way to upgrade AI-generated lyrics.

Go Deeper

The concept of object writing comes from Chapter 1 of Pat Pattison's Writing Better Lyrics. His exercise: pick any object (a doorknob, a coffee mug, rain), set a timer for 10 minutes, and write everything it connects to through your senses. Don't edit. Don't filter. This builds the muscle of associating emotions with concrete details — exactly what this lesson teaches.
