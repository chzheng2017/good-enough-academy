Lesson 003: Stability & Instability

Lesson 3 of the series Estimated time: 15 minutes
Mission connection: You know how to diagnose prosody (Lesson 1) and replace weak content with concrete images (Lesson 2). Now comes the structural lever — how the shape of your lyrics creates motion or resolution, even before the listener processes the words. This is why AI lyrics feel flat even when the images are decent: the structure is uniform. No push, no pull, no arrival.

The Architecture of Feeling

Imagine you're stacking blocks. A tower of 4 blocks feels balanced — it could stand there forever. A tower of 3 blocks feels like it's waiting for a fourth. That tilt, that incompleteness, is instability.

In lyrics, instability creates forward motion — the feeling that the song hasn't arrived yet, that something is coming. Stability creates resolution — the feeling of landing, settling, arriving home.

A great song alternates between these. The verse is unstable (pushing forward). The chorus is stable (arriving). That contrast is what makes the chorus feel like a payoff.

"Unstable sections say keep going. Stable sections say you're home. If everything is stable, there's nowhere to go. If everything is unstable, you never arrive."
— Pat Pattison, Songwriting: Writing the Lyrics, Module 2

Your Two Structural Levers

You control stability with two simple tools:

Lever Stable Unstable
Number of lines Even (2, 4, 6) — feels balanced, complete Odd (3, 5, 7) — feels unfinished, leans forward
Line lengths Matched — lines are similar length, creating symmetry Mismatched — one short line among long ones (or vice versa) creates a stumble, a catch

That's it. Two dials. But they're remarkably powerful.

Case Study 1: Billie Eilish — "when the party's over"

This song is a masterclass in structural contrast. Look at how the sections feel different because of their shape:

Verse — 4 lines (even = stable frame), but matched lengths create restrained calm Don't you know I'm no good for you? I've learned to lose, you can't afford to Tore my shirt to stop you bleedin' But nothin' ever stops you leavin'

(Full lyrics on Genius)

Four lines, roughly matched lengths (8–9 syllables each). This feels contained, restrained — like someone holding it together. The even number gives it balance. The content is emotional, but the structure keeps it measured.

Pre-Chorus — 1 line (odd = maximally unstable) Quiet when I'm coming home and I'm on my own

One single line. That's it. One line standing alone is the most unstable structure possible — it demands that something come next. It's a breath held. The length of the line (13 syllables) sprawls compared to the tight verse lines, adding to the sense that control is slipping.

Chorus — 2 lines (even = stable), with repetition sealing the resolution I could lie, say I like it like that, like it like that I could lie, say I like it like that, like it like that

Two lines, nearly identical. Maximum stability — balanced, paired, and repeated. After the teetering single-line pre-chorus, this landing feels inevitable. The repetition reinforces the stability: we've arrived.

The structural journey

Verse (4 lines, controlled) → Pre-Chorus (1 line, teetering) → Chorus (2 lines, landed). The emotion escalates not because the words get louder, but because the structure destabilizes and then resolves. That's prosody.

Case Study 2: Arctic Monkeys — "Do I Wanna Know?"

Alex Turner uses wildly uneven line lengths to create a feeling of obsessive, spiraling thought — then snaps to short lines in the chorus for relief.

Verse — long, sprawling lines that keep expanding Have you got colour in your cheeks? ← 8 syllables Do you ever get that fear that you can't shift the type ← 15 syllables That sticks around like summat in your teeth? ← 10 syllables Are there some aces up your sleeve? ← 8 syllables Have you no idea that you're in deep? ← 10 syllables I've dreamt about you nearly every night this week ← 13 syllables How many secrets can you keep? ← 8 syllables 'Cause there's this tune I found that makes me think of you somehow an' I play it on repeat ← these three short phrases tumble out Until I fall asleep, spillin' drinks on my settee ← 14 syllables, spillover

(Full lyrics on Genius)

Count the lines: 11 (odd). The line lengths yo-yo between 8 and 15 syllables. Some lines sprawl into the next. The whole verse feels like someone who can't stop talking, whose thoughts keep tumbling out — because the structure mimics that restlessness. This is instability in action.

Chorus — short, fragmented lines that keep breaking Crawlin' back to you ← 5 syllables Ever thought of callin' when ← 7 syllables You've had a few? ← 4 syllables — snap 'Cause I always do ← 5 syllables Maybe I'm too ← 4 syllables Busy bein' yours ← 4 syllables To fall for somebody new ← 7 syllables Now I've thought it through ← 5 syllables Crawlin' back to you ← 5 syllables

Nine lines (odd — still unstable, because the longing isn't resolved). But the line lengths have completely changed: from 8–15 syllables down to 4–7. The short, clipped phrases feel like gasps. The contrast with the sprawling verse creates a structural shift the listener feels even without analyzing it.

When both sections are unstable

Not every chorus needs to be stable. In "Do I Wanna Know?," the chorus is structurally unstable too — because the song's emotion is unresolved longing. The character hasn't arrived anywhere. The structure matches the feeling. That's prosody: the shape tells the truth.

Case Study 3: SZA — "Kill Bill"

Now look at how line count and length work together across the bridge to create the most intense moment in the song:

Verse 1 — 3 lines (odd = unstable, pushing forward) I'm still a fan even though I was salty Hate to see you with some other broad, know you happy Hate to see you happy if I'm not the one drivin'

Three lines — odd. The thoughts spiral and contradict ("I'm a fan" → "I hate to see you happy"). The odd count leaves the section hanging, leaning into what comes next.

Chorus — 4 lines (even = stable arrival) I might kill my ex, not the best idea ← confession + self-awareness in one line His new girlfriend's next, how'd I get here? I might kill my ex, I still love him though Rather be in jail than alone

(Full lyrics on Genius)

Four lines — even. Despite the extreme content, the structure is stable. It feels like a statement of fact, a resolution (even if the resolution is dark). The even count says: "I've made up my mind."

Bridge — 8 lines (even), but the last line detonates I did it all for love I did it all on no drugs I did all of this sober I did it all for us, oh I did it all for love I did all of this on no drugs I did all of this sober Don't you know I did it all for us? ← then whispered: "I'm gon' kill your ass tonight"

The bridge is 8 repetitive, matched-length lines — maximally stable structure. The repetition of "I did it all" has a hypnotic, almost religious quality. But then the whispered parenthetical "I'm gon' kill your ass tonight" breaks the pattern. That single deviation from the stable structure is shocking precisely because everything around it was so controlled.

Why AI Misses This

AI-generated lyrics almost always produce:

  • 4-line verses, 4-line choruses — everything is even, everything is stable
  • Uniform line lengths — every line is 8–10 syllables, no variation
  • No structural contrast between sections — the verse and chorus have the same shape

This is why AI lyrics feel flat even when the words are decent. There's no journey — no push and pull, no tilt and landing. The structure says "nothing is changing," so the listener feels nothing is changing.

The fix for your Suno workflow

After writing (or generating) lyrics, count the lines in each section and note their syllable lengths. Then ask:

  • Is there a structural difference between verse and chorus?
  • Does the verse push forward (odd line count, or mismatched lengths)?
  • Does the chorus arrive (even line count, or matched lengths)?
  • Is there at least one surprise — a short line among long ones, or an extra line that breaks the pattern?

If the answer to any of these is "no," restructure before you paste into Suno.

Your Exercise

Take a set of AI-generated lyrics (or use one you've already generated for Suno). Then:

  1. Count lines per section. Write down: Verse = __ lines, Chorus = __ lines, Bridge = __ lines.
  2. Count syllables per line. Are the lines all the same length? Note the shortest and longest.
  3. Identify the structural journey. Is there contrast between sections? Does the verse push forward? Does the chorus land?
  4. Restructure one section. Try one of these:
    • If your verse is 4 lines, cut it to 3 — feel the difference?
    • If all lines are the same length, shorten the last line dramatically
    • If your pre-chorus doesn't exist, add a single short line between verse and chorus

Paste the original and restructured version into Suno and listen. The AI music generator will adapt to the structural change — you'll hear the difference.

Key takeaway: The shape of your lyrics — how many lines, how long each one is — controls whether a section pushes forward or lands. Verses should feel unstable (odd line counts, varied lengths). Choruses should feel stable (even counts, matched lengths). The contrast between them is what makes a song feel like a journey instead of a flat road.
